My research on seventeenth-century Britain examines how people understood time and the future during political crisis. I focus on the execution of Charles I in 1649 and the responses of astrologers, historians, and newsbook authors to a rupture that made established expectations newly uncertain.

By reading almanacs, newsbooks, and histories across time, I trace how writers adapted familiar ideas about providence, monarchy, generations, and cyclical rebellion to explain the regicide. This work underpins my current book manuscript and has appeared in [*Historical Research*](https://doi.org/10.1093/hisres/htab029) and a [Brill volume on monarchy in early modern Europe](https://doi.org/10.1163/9789004694149_005).

A related strand examines how ideas about place shape political possibility. My MSc research analysed the geographical imaginations embedded in negotiations over the Antarctic and Outer Space Treaties, connecting early modern questions about order and authority to the governance of shared spaces.
